The aperture is a ring of overlapping blades inside the lens that opens and closes to change the
size of the hole light passes through. It does two things at once, which is what makes it the most
interesting of the three controls.

## The numbering

Apertures are written as <GlossaryLink term="f-number" />s — f/1.4, f/2, f/2.8, f/4, f/5.6, f/8,
f/11, f/16 — and the sequence looks arbitrary until you know where it comes from.

The number is a ratio: the lens's <GlossaryLink term="focal-length" /> divided by the width of the
opening. Because it is a division, **a smaller number means a wider opening**.

The steps go up by a factor of √2, which halves the *area* of the hole each time and so halves the
light. Each step is one <GlossaryLink term="stop" />.

<Callout type="tip">
  The ratio is why f/4 admits the same light on any lens, wide or telephoto. That is the entire
  point of the system, and it is worth the awkward numbering.
</Callout>

## The second job

A wider opening also narrows the band of distance that comes out sharp — the
<GlossaryLink term="depth-of-field" />.

Wide open, a portrait has sharp eyes and a background dissolved into
<GlossaryLink term="bokeh" />. Stopped down to f/16, a landscape is sharp from the rocks at your
feet to the hills behind.

Three things make depth of field shallower:

1. A wider aperture.
2. A longer focal length.
3. Standing closer to the subject.

They compound. A long lens, wide open, close up, gives a sliver of sharpness a few centimetres
deep.

## It is not symmetrical

Roughly a third of the sharp zone falls in front of the focus point and two thirds behind it.

The practical consequence: for a group of people at slightly different distances, focus on someone
in the front row rather than the middle. More of the depth will land where you need it.

## Diffraction, and why f/22 is not the answer

Stopping down further and further does not keep improving sharpness. Past roughly f/11 on most
lenses, light bending around the edges of the small opening starts to soften the whole image.

The sharpest aperture on a typical lens is somewhere around f/5.6 to f/8. Use f/16 or f/22 when you
genuinely need the depth, not as a default.

<KeyTakeaways>

- A smaller f-number means a wider opening and more light.
- Wider apertures give shallower depth of field; narrower ones give more.
- Focal length and subject distance change depth of field as much as aperture does.
- Very narrow apertures soften the image through diffraction.

</KeyTakeaways>
