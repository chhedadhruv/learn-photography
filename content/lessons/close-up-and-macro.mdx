<GlossaryLink term="macro" /> means photographing at very close range — strictly, rendering the
subject at life size or larger on the sensor.

Every problem in photography gets worse up close, and in a way that surprises people the first time.

## Depth of field collapses

At normal distances, f/8 gives you metres of <GlossaryLink term="depth-of-field" />. At macro
distances the same aperture gives you a millimetre or two.

Two consequences:

- Stop down further than feels right — f/11 to f/16 is normal, diffraction accepted as the lesser
  problem.
- Which plane you focus on becomes a compositional decision, not a technicality. On an insect, that
  usually means the eyes.

## Focus by moving

At these distances, turning the focus ring changes magnification as much as focus, and the two fight
each other.

The technique that works: set the focus roughly, then move the whole camera slowly forwards and
backwards until the plane you want snaps in. On a tripod, a focusing rail does this precisely.

## Light disappears

Close up, the lens itself blocks light, and stopping down to f/16 costs several more
<GlossaryLink term="stop" />s. Between them, macro is usually short of light even outdoors in
daylight.

The answers: a tripod and a long exposure for anything that will hold still, or flash for anything
that will not.

<Callout type="warning">
  A subject that looks perfectly still is usually not. A breeze you cannot feel moves a flower
  several millimetres, which at this magnification is a great deal.
</Callout>

## Starting without a macro lens

- **Extension tubes** sit between camera and lens and let it focus closer. No glass, so no quality
  loss, and inexpensive.
- **Close-up filters** screw onto the front like a magnifying glass. Cheapest, and softer at the
  edges.
- **Reversing rings** mount a lens backwards. Very cheap, very effective, and awkward to use.

All three will tell you whether you enjoy this before you spend on a dedicated lens.

<KeyTakeaways>

- Depth of field at macro distances is measured in millimetres.
- Focus by moving the camera, not by turning the ring.
- Close-up work is short of light even in daylight; plan for support or flash.
- Extension tubes are a cheap way to find out whether you like it.

</KeyTakeaways>
