When a photograph disappoints, the usual reason is not that something is missing. It is that too
much is present.

Your eye ignores clutter automatically, concentrating on whatever you are interested in. A camera
records all of it at equal weight, which is why scenes so often look busier in a photograph than
they did in life.

## The edges

Most distractions live at the edges, because that is where you were not looking.

Before pressing the button, run your eye deliberately around the border of the frame. Half-cropped
objects, a bright corner, a stray limb — these pull attention outward, and they are all fixable in
the second before the exposure.

## The outline

Then look at the subject's edge against whatever is behind it. The classic failure is a pole or
branch apparently growing out of someone's head. A step to either side fixes it.

<Callout type="warning">
  A bright patch anywhere in the frame will take attention before the subject does, however
  interesting the subject is. Brightness beats content.
</Callout>

## Ways to subtract

- **Move closer.** The most direct answer, and usually the right one.
- **Change <GlossaryLink term="viewpoint" />.** Crouching can put a messy background behind a wall
  or against the sky.
- **Open the <GlossaryLink term="aperture" />.** A softer background is a quieter one — though it
  hides clutter rather than removing it.
- **Wait.** People move. Cars pass. Cloud shadows travel.

## The honest test

Ask what the photograph is of, in one short phrase. Then ask what every part of the frame is
contributing to that.

Anything that cannot answer is a candidate for removal — and if it cannot be removed, that is
usually a sign to find a different photograph rather than to take this one anyway.

<KeyTakeaways>

- Your eye filters clutter; the camera does not.
- Check the edges and the subject's outline before each frame.
- A bright area takes attention regardless of what the subject is.
- Name what the photograph is of, then justify everything else in it.

</KeyTakeaways>
