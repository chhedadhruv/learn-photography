<GlossaryLink term="iso" /> describes how strongly the camera amplifies what the sensor collected.
Doubling it — 100 to 200, 200 to 400 — buys one <GlossaryLink term="stop" /> of brightness.

It is the only one of the three controls that does not change how much light actually arrives. It
turns up the volume on what did.

## Why that matters

Amplifying a signal amplifies whatever else is in it. The result is
<GlossaryLink term="noise" />: a speckled texture that shows first and worst in the shadows,
because that is where the signal was weakest.

This is why ISO is the control to reach for last. Aperture and shutter speed collect more light;
ISO makes do with less.

<Callout type="note">
  Raising ISO does not create noise so much as reveal it. The same noise was always there — the
  amplification just brings it up alongside the picture.
</Callout>

## When to raise it anyway

Constantly. The alternative to a noisy photograph is usually a blurred one or no photograph at
all, and noise is by far the least bad of those.

The order that works:

1. Open the aperture as far as the depth of field allows.
2. Slow the shutter as far as the subject and your hands allow.
3. Then raise the ISO to cover the rest.

If steps one and two are exhausted and the photograph is still dark, ISO is not a compromise —
it is the answer.

## Underexposing is not a workaround

A common instinct is to keep ISO low, accept a dark photograph, and brighten it afterwards.

This is worse. Brightening in software amplifies the signal in exactly the way raising ISO would
have, and usually with less information to work with. Many cameras apply amplification before the
noisiest part of their own electronics, which software cannot do.

Expose properly at a higher ISO instead.

## Knowing your own camera

Sensors differ enormously, and published figures are less useful than your own eyes. Spend twenty
minutes photographing the same dim scene at every ISO your camera offers, look at the results at
the size you actually use, and find the point where the noise starts to bother you.

That number is worth more than any general advice, because it is about your camera and your
tolerance.

<KeyTakeaways>

- ISO amplifies the signal rather than collecting more light.
- Amplification reveals noise, worst in the shadows.
- Use aperture and shutter first, then raise ISO to cover the remainder.
- Underexposing and brightening later is worse, not better, than a higher ISO.

</KeyTakeaways>
