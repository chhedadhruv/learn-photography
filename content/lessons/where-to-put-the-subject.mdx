The first compositional decision is where in the frame the subject sits. Most beginners never make
it: the subject ends up in the middle because that is where the focus point was.

## The rule of thirds

Divide the frame into a three-by-three grid. The <GlossaryLink term="rule-of-thirds" /> suggests
placing the subject on one of those lines, or at an intersection.

It works because it leaves the subject somewhere to sit. A person on a third with space in front of
them reads as though they are going somewhere; the same person centred reads as a specimen.

Most cameras will draw the grid in the viewfinder. Turn it on.

<Callout type="note">
  It is a starting point, not a law. Almost every rule in composition is a description of what
  usually works, dressed up as an instruction.
</Callout>

## When centring is right

- Symmetry. A reflection, a doorway, a face straight on.
- Confrontation. A centred portrait is direct in a way an off-centre one is not.
- When the surroundings add nothing and the subject should fill the frame.

The failure is not centring. The failure is centring without deciding to.

## What the space is doing

The more useful question than "where does the subject go" is "what is the rest of the frame for".

<GlossaryLink term="negative-space" /> — sky, wall, water, shadow — isolates a subject, suggests
scale, and gives the eye somewhere to rest. But it only works if the space is genuinely empty. A
cluttered background is not negative space; it is competition.

## Room to move, room to look

Two conventions that are worth following until you have a reason not to:

- Give a moving subject space in front of them rather than behind.
- Give a person looking off-frame space on the side they are looking towards.

Both make the photograph feel less cramped, for the same reason: they leave room for the thing that
is about to happen.

<KeyTakeaways>

- Decide where the subject goes rather than letting the focus point decide.
- The rule of thirds is a prompt, not a law; centring is right for symmetry and confrontation.
- Empty space isolates a subject — but only when it is genuinely empty.
- Leave space in the direction a subject is moving or looking.

</KeyTakeaways>
