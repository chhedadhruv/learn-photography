Light is not colourless. Tungsten bulbs are orange, overcast sky is blue, open shade is bluer still.

Your eyes adapt so completely that you rarely notice — walk from a lit room into daylight and within
seconds both look neutral. A sensor does not adapt at all. It records the colour that was actually
there, which is why indoor photographs come out orange and shaded ones blue.

## The scale

<GlossaryLink term="colour-temperature" /> is measured in kelvin.

| Source | Approximate |
| --- | --- |
| Candle | 1,900K |
| Tungsten bulb | 3,200K |
| Sunrise, sunset | 3,500K |
| Midday sun | 5,500K |
| Overcast | 6,500K |
| Open shade | 7,500K+ |

The confusing part: *lower* numbers are warmer. The scale describes the colour of the light source,
so telling the camera "the light is 7,500K" means "the light is blue" — and it compensates by
warming the photograph.

## Setting it

**Auto** is good, and occasionally confidently wrong — usually when one colour dominates the frame.
A photograph of a red wall may come out oddly green, because the camera assumed the redness was in
the light.

**Presets** — daylight, cloudy, tungsten, shade — are more reliable when you know the light.

**Custom** means photographing something neutral and telling the camera to use it as the reference.
The most accurate, and the slowest.

<Callout type="tip">
  Shooting <GlossaryLink term="raw" /> makes this nearly a non-problem. White balance is recorded as
  a note rather than baked in, so it can be set afterwards as though you had chosen correctly.
</Callout>

## Correct is not always right

Neutral is a starting point, not the goal. A sunset corrected to neutrality is no longer a sunset;
warm firelight corrected to neutrality looks like a fluorescent-lit room.

Correct first so you know where neutral is. Then deviate deliberately.

## Mixed light

The genuinely awkward case: a room lit by both a window and a lamp. One correction cannot fix both,
so one part of the photograph will always be off.

The options are to turn one source off, to move the subject so one source dominates, or to accept it.
There is no setting that solves it.

<KeyTakeaways>

- Your eyes adapt to the colour of light and sensors do not.
- Lower kelvin numbers are warmer; setting a higher number warms the photograph.
- RAW makes white balance a decision you can defer entirely.
- Correct to neutral first, then deviate on purpose.

</KeyTakeaways>
