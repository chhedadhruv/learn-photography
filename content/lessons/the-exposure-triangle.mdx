Three controls decide how bright a photograph is. Any of them will brighten or darken it, and each
one also changes something no other control can.

That second effect is why the choice matters. If brightness were all that mattered, you could pick
any of them at random.

## Everything is measured in stops

A <GlossaryLink term="stop" /> is a doubling or halving of light. It is the unit that makes the
three controls interchangeable.

| Control | One stop more light |
| --- | --- |
| <GlossaryLink term="shutter-speed" /> | 1/500 → 1/250 |
| <GlossaryLink term="aperture" /> | f/8 → f/5.6 |
| <GlossaryLink term="iso" /> | ISO 200 → ISO 400 |

Because they are the same size, they trade evenly. Give up one stop on the shutter and take it
back on the aperture and the brightness is identical.

<Callout type="tip">
  Thinking in stops rather than in numbers is most of learning exposure. The numbers are irregular
  and awkward; the stops are all the same size.
</Callout>

## Reciprocal exposures

Because a stop given up on one control can be taken back on another, many different combinations
produce exactly the same brightness:

| Aperture | Shutter | ISO | Brightness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| f/2.8 | 1/1000 | 100 | identical |
| f/4 | 1/500 | 100 | identical |
| f/5.6 | 1/250 | 100 | identical |
| f/5.6 | 1/1000 | 400 | identical |

These are not the same photograph. The first has a soft background and freezes movement. The third
has more in focus. The fourth freezes movement and keeps the background, at the cost of some
<GlossaryLink term="noise" />.

Choosing between them *is* photography. The exposure is just the constraint.

## What each one costs

- **Aperture** buys light, and spends <GlossaryLink term="depth-of-field" />. Wider is brighter
  and shallower.
- **Shutter speed** buys light, and spends the ability to freeze movement. Slower is brighter and
  more smeared.
- **ISO** buys light, and spends cleanliness. Higher is brighter and noisier.

There is no free option. Every stop of brightness is bought with something.

## An order that usually works

1. Decide what the photograph depends on — frozen movement, or a soft background.
2. Set that control first, and do not compromise on it.
3. Set the other of the two to bring the exposure close.
4. Use ISO to cover whatever is left.

ISO last, because it is the only one that does not change how much light actually arrives — it
just amplifies what did.

<KeyTakeaways>

- Aperture, shutter speed and ISO all change brightness, and nothing else does.
- All three are measured in stops, so they trade evenly against each other.
- Many combinations give the same brightness but different photographs.
- Set the control the photograph depends on first, and use ISO last.
