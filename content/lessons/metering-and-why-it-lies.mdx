Your camera measures the light before it chooses an exposure. That measurement rests on one
assumption, and understanding the assumption explains nearly every automatic exposure mistake you
will see.

## The assumption

A meter assumes that whatever it is pointed at averages out to
<GlossaryLink term="middle-grey" /> — a tone reflecting about 18% of the light falling on it.

Point a camera at anything and it will choose an exposure that renders that thing as middle grey.
Point it at a white wall and it gives you a grey wall. Point it at a black cat and it gives you a
grey cat.

For most scenes this works, because most scenes contain a mixture of light and dark that genuinely
does average out near the middle.

## Where it fails

Two situations, and they fail in opposite directions.

**A bright background.** A person photographed against a bright sky: the meter reads mostly sky,
decides the scene is very bright, and cuts the exposure. The sky comes out correct and the face
comes out dark.

**A uniformly bright or dark scene.** A snow field is not mid-grey — it is snow. The meter reads
it, renders it as mid-grey, and hands you a photograph of dirty slush. A black cat filling the
frame comes out grey for the same reason.

<Callout type="warning">
  The second case is the one that catches people, because there is no bright background to blame
  and every metering mode agrees with every other. The scene simply is not mid-grey.
</Callout>

## Metering modes

How much of the frame the camera measures:

- **Average** or **evaluative** reads the whole frame. Right most of the time.
- **Centre-weighted** reads the whole frame but favours the middle. A reasonable default.
- **Spot** reads a small area only. Right when the subject is much brighter or darker than
  everything around it.

Changing the mode fixes the *first* failure — measure less of that bright sky and the reading
improves. It does nothing for the second, because when the whole frame is snow it does not matter
how much of it you measure.

## Exposure compensation

<GlossaryLink term="exposure-compensation" /> tells the camera its reading is wrong and by how
much, without leaving an automatic mode. Set +1 and every photograph comes out one
<GlossaryLink term="stop" /> brighter than the meter wanted.

Rough starting points:

| Scene | Compensation |
| --- | --- |
| Snow, sand, a white wall | +1 to +2 |
| A subject against a bright sky | +1 to +2 |
| A dark subject filling the frame | −1 to −2 |
| A spotlit subject on a dark stage | −1 to −2 |

It only does anything in an automatic mode. In full manual the camera is not choosing the
exposure, so there is nothing to compensate — you simply set what you want.

<KeyTakeaways>

- Meters assume the scene averages to middle grey, and expose to make it so.
- A bright background makes the camera underexpose the subject; the metering mode can fix it.
- A uniformly bright scene comes out grey, and only compensation can fix it.
- Compensation does nothing in manual, because nothing is being chosen for you.

</KeyTakeaways>
