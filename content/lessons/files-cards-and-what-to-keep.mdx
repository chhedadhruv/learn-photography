The least interesting part of photography, and the one that costs people the most when they get it
wrong.

## RAW or JPEG

Two ways the camera can save what the sensor recorded.

A <GlossaryLink term="jpeg" /> is finished. The camera has applied its own white balance,
contrast and sharpening, thrown away what it did not need, and compressed the result into a small
file anyone can open.

A <GlossaryLink term="raw" /> file is the sensor's data with the interpretation left off. It is
not really a picture yet — it needs software to become one — but it holds far more of the original
range.

| | RAW | JPEG |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Room to correct | Large | Small |
| File size | Several times bigger | Small |
| Ready to share | No | Yes |
| White balance | Changeable afterwards | Baked in |

The practical rule: shoot RAW when the light is difficult or the photograph matters, JPEG when
neither is true. Most cameras will record both simultaneously, which costs card space and settles
the argument.

<Callout type="note">
  RAW is not a magic recovery tool. It buys perhaps a stop of latitude at each end — real, but far
  less than getting the exposure right in the first place.
</Callout>

## Memory cards

Two numbers matter, and only one of them usually does.

**Capacity** is straightforward. **Speed** matters if you shoot bursts or video; for single
photographs almost any current card keeps up.

The more important advice is about how many. Cards fail, and they fail suddenly and completely.
Two smaller cards cost you half a day when one dies; one large card costs you the whole day.

- Format cards in the camera, not on a computer.
- Format rather than deleting individual files.
- Replace a card that has ever behaved oddly. They are cheap and the photographs are not.

## The backup habit

One copy is not a backup. Two copies on the same computer are not a backup either — they fail
together.

The workable minimum: the photographs on your computer, and a copy somewhere that is not your
computer. An external drive kept elsewhere, or a cloud service, or both.

Do it on the day you take the photographs. The gap between shooting and copying is where losses
happen.

<KeyTakeaways>

- RAW keeps more range and survives correction; JPEG is finished and ready to share.
- Shoot RAW when the light is hard or the photograph matters.
- Carry more than one memory card, and format them in the camera.
- Copy photographs off the card the same day, to somewhere that is not your computer.

</KeyTakeaways>
