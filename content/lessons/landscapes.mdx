Landscapes are forgiving technically and demanding compositionally. Nothing moves, the light is free,
and you can take as long as you like — which leaves nothing to blame but the picture.

## Settings

**Aperture** around f/8 to f/11. That is where most lenses are sharpest and where
<GlossaryLink term="depth-of-field" /> is deep enough for a scene with a foreground. Going to f/22
buys a little more depth and loses sharpness everywhere to diffraction.

**ISO** at base — usually 100. There is rarely a reason to raise it if the camera is supported.

**Shutter speed** whatever the other two leave. On a tripod it does not matter.

## Where to focus

Focusing on the horizon is the common mistake: everything behind the horizon is already at infinity,
so all the depth of field behind your focus point is wasted.

Focus roughly a third of the way into the scene. For precision, use the
<GlossaryLink term="hyperfocal-distance" /> — focus there and everything from half that distance to
infinity is acceptably sharp.

## The tripod

Not for low light so much as for discipline. A tripod slows you down, which is the single most
reliable way to improve a landscape photograph — it makes you look at the edges of the frame before
committing rather than after.

It also lets you use base ISO and the sharpest aperture regardless of how dim it gets.

## Foreground

The difference between a landscape that works and a view that was better in life is almost always a
foreground.

A flat wide shot of hills flattens further in a photograph. Something near the camera — rocks, a
gate, water, flowers — gives the eye a starting point and implies the depth the flat image lost. See
[leading the eye](/learn/leading-the-eye).

<Callout type="tip">
  The foreground is usually why landscape photographers get up early and walk. The light matters,
  but so does having time to find the near thing.
</Callout>

## Timing

Midday landscape light is flat and unhelpful. Low sun rakes across the land and reveals its shape.

Weather is more interesting than clear skies. A blank blue sky is the least interesting sky
available, and clearing storms are the most.

<KeyTakeaways>

- f/8 to f/11 at base ISO covers most landscapes.
- Focusing on the horizon wastes depth of field; focus a third in.
- A tripod's real value is that it slows you down.
- A foreground is usually what separates a photograph from a view.

</KeyTakeaways>
